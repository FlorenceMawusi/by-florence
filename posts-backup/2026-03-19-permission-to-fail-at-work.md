# Permission to Fail at Work
_How removing the pressure to be perfect changed everything._

[![Man rubbing his face in front of laptop.](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1758598497429-6eb3895d5bfa?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHw2fHx3b3JrJTIwZmFpbHxlbnwwfHx8fDE3NzM5MjE0NjB8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)](<https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1758598497429-6eb3895d5bfa?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHw2fHx3b3JrJTIwZmFpbHxlbnwwfHx8fDE3NzM5MjE0NjB8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080>)Photo by [Vitaly Gariev](<https://unsplash.com/@silverkblack>) on [Unsplash](<https://unsplash.com>)

Let me tell you something kinda wild about the workplace.

When a company hires you, they’re not just hiring your resume. They’re hiring your brain. The weird way you connect random dots. That gut feeling you get when something’s off before anyone can prove it. Your imagination. Your creativity. Your chaos.

They’re hiring a _whole entire human being_.

But then you actually show up. And slowly — like so slowly you almost don’t notice — the environment starts trimming you down like a bonsai tree that didn’t ask for a haircut.

Don’t fail. Don’t take too long. Don’t go down that rabbit hole. Don’t experiment. Don’t be “still figuring it out” in public. Just… ship. Perform. Give your little update in standup like a good employee.

And so you do what any smart person does when the room punishes curiosity.

You shrink.

You stop bringing up the wild idea. You stop saying “I don’t know yet but I’m onto something.” You start running every thought through the filter of “will this make me look dumb” instead of “is this actually worth exploring.”

You start _performing_ productivity instead of actually thinking.

And here’s the funny part — the company thinks they’re winning. They see the tickets closed. The updates delivered. The standups completed on time. System’s working, right?

Lol. No.

What they’re actually getting is the watered-down, filter-everything, play-it-safe version of you. The one who learned to scan the room and deliver whatever won’t get them flagged. The one who stopped taking risks months ago because the last time something didn’t work out, they had to stand in front of a screen and basically explain why they deserve to still be here.

My dear. That’s not productivity. That’s compliance in a productivity costume. And it’s not giving.

* * *

Here’s the thing though.

Some of us didn’t learn how to solve problems in a classroom or a corporate training. We learned it in life. Like, actual life life.

We learned it by facing stuff that had no clear answer. No manual. No senior engineer to pair-program with. Just us and a problem that looked absolutely impossible. And we kept trying anyway. Tried one thing. Didn’t work. Tried another. Also flopped. Tried a third thing that was honestly a little unhinged. And eventually — maybe on the fifteenth or twentieth attempt — something clicked.

And the thing is? Attempts one through nineteen weren’t failures. They were the homework. Each one taught us something we literally could not have learned any other way.

That’s how real problem-solving works. It’s messy and slow and embarrassing sometimes and you look kinda lost in the middle of it. But the breakthrough? It doesn’t come from attempt number one. It comes from the patience to keep going when it looks like nothing’s working.

And THAT is exactly the energy that dies when a workplace makes failure feel like a verdict instead of a step.

Like bestie. I didn’t survive all that personal growth — years of trying, failing, unlearning, rebuilding — just to come to work and pretend I figured everything out on the first try.

* * *

The best ideas I’ve ever had didn’t come from pressure. They came from space. From someone trusting my process even when I had absolutely nothing to show for it yet.

Pressure doesn’t make anyone sharper. It just makes people faster at playing it safe. And then we wonder why nobody’s innovating. Maybe it’s because the room doesn’t feel safe enough to try.

Nobody — and I mean nobody — does their best thinking when they’re not allowed to get anything wrong. We all need room. Room to sit with a problem. To try something that might completely break. To go “huh, that was a disaster… interesting” and not have it become a whole performance review moment.

That’s literally how discovery works.

* * *

And can we talk about perfectionism for a second? Because I think it’s the silent killer in all of this.

Here’s how the loop works. You’re scared to fail. So you try to be perfect. But perfect takes forever. So now you’re procrastinating. And while you’re procrastinating, you’re people-pleasing — saying yes to things, performing busyness, looking productive — because at least _that_ feels like you’re doing something right. Meanwhile the actual work? The real thinking? It’s frozen. Because you’re too afraid to start something you might not get right.

And here’s the plot twist.

Perfectionists fail too. All the time. Because you don’t know what you don’t know. You can triple-check your code, rehearse your presentation forty times, plan every detail — and something will still surprise you. Something will still break. That’s just… how things work.

So if you’re going to fail anyway — perfectionist or not — then what exactly is the perfectionism protecting you from?

Nothing. It’s just fear in a fancy outfit.

But when you drop the “I must get it right” mindset? Something kind of magical happens. You actually move faster. You ask more questions because you’re not pretending to already know. You’re open to suggestions because your ego isn’t wrapped around being the person who never needed help. You try things sooner because you’re not waiting for the “perfect” moment that doesn’t exist.

Dropping perfectionism doesn’t make you sloppy. It makes you _available_. Available to learn, to adapt, to actually grow.

And isn’t that what we’re all supposed to be doing at work anyway?

* * *

And while we’re here — let’s talk about standups.

You know what a standup _could_ be? A room full of people going “yo I found something weird yesterday and I don’t fully get it yet” and everyone leaning in like ooooh tell me more.

Instead it’s a daily courtroom. What did you do. What will you do. Any blockers. Next person. Next person. Meeting over.

Where’s the thinking?? Where’s the space for someone to say “I went down a whole path that led nowhere BUT I learned something wild along the way”? Where’s the humanity??

Standups could literally be the most interesting 15 minutes of your day. Instead they’re the most stressful. And I think that says a lot.

* * *

Here’s what I keep landing on.

When you build a culture of fear — and I don’t even mean loud scary fear, I mean that quiet kind where everyone just feels _watched_ and _evaluated_ all the time — you don’t get great work. You get people spending 80% of their energy managing how they’re perceived and 20% actually doing the thing you hired them to do.

You hired a mind. A whole, living, sometimes-chaotic, beautifully-wired mind with its own rhythm and its own weird brilliant way of getting to the answer.

Let it cook.

Or keep getting the filtered version and wondering why everything your team makes feels… mid.

* * *

And okay — I know what some people are thinking. “So what, we just let people do nothing?”

No. But here’s the thing.

When someone on your team isn’t producing — like genuinely not delivering — the answer usually isn’t more pressure. More check-ins. More “where are we on this.” Because that almost never fixes it. It just makes them better at hiding.

If someone’s not working, there’s almost always something deeper going on. Maybe it’s procrastination because they’re paralyzed by perfectionism. Maybe they don’t feel fairly paid and they’ve mentally checked out. Maybe they’re burned out and running on fumes. Maybe they’re working another job because this one doesn’t cover rent. Maybe they’re dealing with something at home that they don’t feel safe enough to mention.

There’s usually a root. And it’s usually not laziness.

But getting to that root? That takes a different kind of conversation. The kind most managers aren’t trained for and honestly might not be ready for. It takes sitting down and asking “what’s actually going on” and meaning it. Not as a performance review. Not as a warning. Just… human to human.

And yeah, that conversation might be uncomfortable. It might reveal something that requires real change — a pay adjustment, a role shift, a schedule change, a hard truth neither of you wanted to face.

But oh boy. If you have the courage to go there? Both of you come out so different. Better. Closer to something that actually works.

That’s leadership. Not the spreadsheet kind. The human kind.

* * *

And one more thing. This one’s for the employees.

You know that feeling when your boss says “good job” and your whole day lights up? And when they don’t say it — or worse, when something goes wrong — your whole day crashes?

Yeah. I know that feeling too.

But here’s what I’m learning. When you remove the perfectionism — when you stop carrying that “I must get it right” energy into everything — something weird happens. The “good job” stops hitting like a drug. And the “bad job” stops hitting like a funeral.

Not because you stopped caring about your work. But because you stopped letting someone else’s two words be the whole measurement of your worth.

When you’re not chasing perfection, a bug is just a bug. Not a verdict on your intelligence. A project that doesn’t land is just a project that didn’t land. Not proof that you don’t belong here. Your boss’s approval feels nice but it doesn’t make or break your day anymore. Because your sense of worth isn’t sitting on their desk waiting for them to sign off on it.

It’s yours. It was always yours.

And the wild part? That’s usually when the good work actually starts flowing. Because all that energy you were spending on managing how you’re perceived? On rehearsing what to say in standup? On dreading the feedback? That energy is free now. And it goes straight into the thing you were hired to do in the first place.

Think. Create. Build. Be human about it.

* * *

_The most expensive thing a company can do is hire brilliant people and then make them too afraid to think._

_The second most expensive thing? Not even realizing they did it._
