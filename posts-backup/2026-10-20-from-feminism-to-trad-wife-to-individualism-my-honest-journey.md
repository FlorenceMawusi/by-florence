# Finding Feminism 

## My University

Going to a school like the one I went to in Ghana teaches you things you wouldn’t have learnt otherwise. My school, Ashesi University, focused on building the next generation of African leaders so this was what informed its curriculum. this curriculum of course did not exclude machine learning when I was a student there (from 2017 to 2021).

## AI and the Humanities 

In the hype of AI even in universities, one might think that the courses that gives a student an upper hand after they graduate are those relating to machine learning, computer science, and Generative AI. I think that’s true to some extent. Although I believe that the Humanities triumph the computer sciences. Why do I say so? 

Because the sciences teach us what to think. But even more importantly the humanities teach us how to think, which is one of the most crucial skills you can have in this world. 

## Historical Philosophy 

Ashesi university gave me an understanding of historical great texts. From Plato’s allegory of the cave to Machiavelli’s books on war and strategy, I was enriched with new and fascinating ideas. 

I do wish we had studied more african philosophies than those from foreign countries, but nonetheless the information was beneficial. 

We explored concepts such as leadership, means to end vs end to means, community, and morality. 

Through historical texts, we studied social concepts like neocolonialism and feminism. 

## My Intro to Feminism 

Feminism was one interesting ideology I got introduced to in to one of these Humanities classes. It definitely lighted parts of me up, parts that I would agree to because I had a strong need for freedom, for respect of my free will and acknowledgment of the value of my gender. 

Seated within me was a deep need to be understood and accepted for my strengths as well as my quirks, just as males are raised in many households to have this unshakable sense of confidence. This ‘I can do it all’ spirit makes them approach life’s challenges without batting an eye, even when they make mistakes. 

## Admiration for Bold Women 

So I was stoked about feminism and all the things that we learned in class. 

For example, learning about Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, I was mesmerized by her. I thought that she was one of the most confident, bold women I had met. And I found confident, bold women very admirable. 

I realized that I was drawn to such women, women who had, for example, deep voices, or even women who had an edge to them, or were able to express themselves without fear of being judged and didn't have to portray themselves as perfect. 

I even remember that one of my role models was Jennifer Lawrence, because in her movies, she had this fearless outlook on life.

I recently watched The Hunger Games, and it reminded me of her undeniable authenticity and how that shined through, how that emanated from her so much so that she was able to instill hope in all the nine districts in Panem. She was awarded the name her as the Mockingjay and was a beacon for the revolution needed at the time. 

That was her impact in the movie setting, and I still find that impressive.

## Past Shadows

I did not have that boldness back then . I didn't have much of a voice. Although, I was extremely smart and intelligent. 

It was a different ballgame for me when it came to speaking out or just sharing my opinions. 

Expressing what I really thought and what I really believed, or not thinking twice before showing what was really going on for me: these were not in my vocabulary. 

## Growth

I have come a long way now, and this is not the case anymore. 

But it is just interesting to reflect and realize who I was before vs who I am now due to self-awareness. I did lots of reconditioning of habits I had learned since I was young. 

Part of my reserved nature might also be my personality, since my friends tell me I’m one of the calmest persons they’ve met and that I help them emotionally regulate just with my presence. But I think that being a calm person didn't have to affect my ability to radiate that calmness with the world.

## Love for Feminism 

And so yes, passionate I was about all these new concepts I was learning, especially feminism. At that point I had kind of come to a conclusion that being a woman was very restricting in our world, which was true, because being a woman brought a lot of expectations about who I should be and what I should want. And that was very tiring.

And learning about this concept of feminism opened me up to understanding what other women thought about how they had been treated, what they thought was acceptable, and how they should be treated now in our modern era. So it gave me knowledge that I was not alone, and it made me also not think about marriage or having kids. In fact, I wasn't even convinced that I would ever want to have kids at that point because I just felt like it was time for me to be selfish in a good way, because I had been deprived of that as a woman in the world.

# Becoming a Trad Wife Fan

## An Unexpected Encounter

During the COVID-19 pandemic, I spent a lot of time at home. Like many people, I needed ways to occupy myself while going about my daily routine, so I developed the habit of listening to podcasts, audiobooks, and YouTube videos as I worked. Saturday mornings, in particular, were usually reserved for a general household clean, and I would play something in the background to make the work a little less tedious.

It was during one of these ordinary mornings that I heard a man make a rather striking statement: women who did not spend their time having children would regret it later in life.

I was immediately irritated. As someone who had only recently begun exploring feminism and thinking more critically about the expectations placed on women, I found the statement frustrating. It seemed to suggest that a woman’s life could be measured by whether she fulfilled a particular role.

I skipped the video and went on with my day.

However, I kept encountering similar content in the weeks that followed. Eventually, I discovered that the man behind the videos was Jordan Peterson. What began as irritation gradually developed into curiosity. I wanted to understand why these ideas appealed to so many people and whether there was something about traditional values that I had overlooked.

## Discovering Traditional Values

My curiosity led me down a path I had not anticipated. I began exploring the arguments made by Jordan Peterson and other commentators who advocated for traditional values, family life, and more conventional gender roles.

Through their discussions, I encountered perspectives that challenged some of the assumptions I had developed during my introduction to feminism. I began to consider the possibility that building a family could be just as meaningful as pursuing a career, and that there might be value in creating a home, raising children, and sharing life with a committed partner.

The more I listened, the more appealing certain aspects of this lifestyle became.

I was particularly drawn to the idea that life did not have to be an endless exercise in proving oneself. I had grown up believing that strength, independence, and the ability to handle things on my own were important qualities to cultivate. And they were. But somewhere along the way, I had begun to associate being strong with having to do everything myself.

The idea that I could build a life in which I did not always have to carry everything began to resonate with me.

I started to see family life differently. Rather than viewing marriage and motherhood primarily through the lens of the restrictions they might impose on women, I began to appreciate the meaning, companionship, and fulfillment they could offer.

I also became more receptive to the idea of traditional femininity. I wanted to understand what it meant to embrace a softer side of myself, to allow someone else to take the lead sometimes, and to experience the comfort of being able to rely on another person.

For a while, these ideas significantly influenced how I imagined my future. I became more interested in prioritizing family over career and in exploring what it might mean to have a more traditional relationship.

In many ways, it felt like a reversal of my earlier convictions. I had once been drawn to feminism because I wanted the freedom to define my own life without being confined by society’s expectations of womanhood. Now, I was finding something appealing in a lifestyle that appeared to offer a different kind of freedom: the freedom to stop striving, to embrace family life, and to allow myself to be supported.

## **The Appeal of Letting Go**

Looking back, I think part of what attracted me to traditional values was the opportunity to reconsider what strength meant to me.

I had been raised to be strong, capable, and independent. These qualities had served me well, but I was beginning to question whether I always needed to embody them so intensely. I wanted to experience what it felt like not to be responsible for everything, not to have to prove that I could handle every challenge, and not to feel that my worth depended on how much I could accomplish alone.

Traditional relationship dynamics seemed to offer an answer to this desire.

The idea of having a partner who could lead, provide, and create a sense of security appealed to me. So did the possibility of devoting more time to family and less to the relentless pursuit of individual achievement.

At the time, I found these ideas convincing. I could see the beauty in a woman choosing to care for her home and family, just as I could see the beauty in a woman pursuing a demanding career. I was beginning to understand that there were different ways to construct a meaningful life.

However, as I continued exploring these ideas, I gradually began to recognize a distinction that would become increasingly important to me: there is a difference between choosing a particular way of life because it genuinely fulfills you and believing that you ought to live that way because someone else has convinced you that it is the right way.

I had become so interested in understanding traditional values that I had started to imagine them as an answer to some of my own struggles. Yet I had not fully considered whether embracing traditional femininity meant that I had to become a particular kind of woman.

Did I have to be soft all the time? Did I have to want a man to lead in every situation? Did prioritizing family mean that career had to become less important to me? And did being a strong, independent woman necessarily mean that I was resisting my femininity?

I did not have all the answers then.

But these questions would eventually lead me somewhere unexpected: away from the need to identify with a particular ideology and toward a deeper appreciation of individual freedom.

# Landing on Individualism

## A Different Understanding of Freedom

As my interest in traditional values evolved, I became increasingly interested in spirituality. This introduced me to new ways of thinking about life, human nature, and what it means to live authentically.

One idea that particularly resonated with me was the importance of freedom: the freedom to choose, to exercise one’s own judgment, and to decide what kind of life one wants to live.

This idea prompted me to reconsider both feminism and traditional values.

I began to realize that, although these ideologies often presented themselves as competing visions of womanhood, neither had to determine how I lived my life. I could appreciate the arguments made by feminists without agreeing with every feminist perspective. I could recognize the beauty of traditional family life without believing that every woman should pursue it.

More importantly, I began to understand that women, just like men, have the right to make their own choices.

A woman can choose to become a mother, pursue a career, stay at home, build a business, prioritize her relationships, or devote herself to a combination of these things. She can embrace traditional femininity, reject it, or move between different expressions of herself as she grows.

None of these choices automatically makes her more or less of a woman.

What matters is that the choice is genuinely hers.

## Beyond Feminism and Traditionalism

This realization was significant because I had spent years exploring different ideas about how I ought to live. First, feminism helped me recognize the restrictions that society could place on women. Later, traditional values encouraged me to reconsider the importance of family, partnership, and allowing myself to be supported.

Both perspectives offered insights that I found valuable. Yet I began to question why I needed to commit myself entirely to either one.

Why should I have to choose between being independent and being cared for? Why should I have to decide whether I am a career woman or a family-oriented woman, as though these identities cannot coexist? And why should my personality, desires, or ambitions have to fit neatly into a predefined understanding of femininity?

I could be strong and still want support. I could be ambitious and still prioritize my family. I could enjoy leading in some situations and allow someone else to lead in others. I could be calm and gentle without suppressing my opinions, and I could be assertive without feeling that I was somehow abandoning my femininity.

I did not have to become a different person to accommodate a particular ideology.

Instead, I could take what resonated with me from the ideas I encountered, question what did not, and make decisions based on my own understanding of myself.

This was the beginning of a more individualistic outlook.

To me, individualism does not mean that relationships, family, or community are unimportant. Nor does it mean that we should disregard the needs of others in pursuit of our own desires. Rather, it means recognizing that every person has an inner world, a set of needs, and the right to participate in shaping their own life.

We can learn from other people without surrendering our judgment to them. We can value traditions without treating them as unquestionable rules. And we can appreciate ideologies without allowing them to become the sole authority on who we are supposed to be.

## Freedom Within Relationships

Perhaps the most important place where this understanding has influenced me is in how I think about relationships.

I believe that a healthy relationship requires two people who are willing to recognize each other’s individuality. Each person comes into the relationship with their own desires, expectations, boundaries, and understanding of what a fulfilling partnership looks like.

These needs will not always align perfectly. In fact, it would be unrealistic to expect them to.

One person might want a more traditional relationship in which one partner takes the lead in certain areas. The other might prefer a partnership in which decisions are made collaboratively. Neither preference is inherently wrong, provided that both people are free to express what they want and to decide whether the arrangement works for them.

The problem begins when one person’s preferences are treated as rules that the other person must follow.

For example, if someone wants their partner to take responsibility for all the household chores, they are allowed to want that. Their desire does not have to be universally logical or objectively fair to be a genuine preference. It is something they value, and they should be able to communicate it honestly.

However, expressing a desire does not automatically make it an obligation for the other person.

Their partner is equally entitled to decide whether they are willing to meet that expectation. Perhaps they agree to take on all the chores. Perhaps they do not. Perhaps they discuss the arrangement and find a compromise that works for both of them.

The same principle applies to larger decisions about marriage, children, careers, finances, and gender roles.

We are allowed to ask for what we want, and other people are allowed to decide whether they can give it to us. We are allowed to have preferences, and our partners are allowed to have theirs. Neither person should have to erase their own needs simply because the other person has a different vision of how the relationship should work.

Of course, compromise does not mean that every disagreement can be resolved perfectly, or that every request must be accommodated. Sometimes two people want fundamentally different things. In those situations, the answer may be to acknowledge the incompatibility rather than force one person to abandon something important to them.

I find this understanding liberating because it removes the need to establish one universal formula for a successful relationship.

A traditional arrangement can work beautifully for two people who genuinely want it. So can a more egalitarian arrangement. What matters is not whether the relationship conforms to a particular ideology, but whether the people within it have the freedom to choose their roles, communicate their needs, and treat each other with respect.

No one should have to surrender their voice to prove their love.

## The Right to Choose

I do not know exactly what my beliefs will look like a few years from now. I am still learning, questioning, and discovering new things about myself. I would like to remain open to changing my mind when I encounter ideas that challenge what I currently believe.

But there is one principle that feels particularly important to me at this stage of my life: the right to choose.

I do not want to live according to a set of rules simply because they have been presented as the correct way to be a woman. I do not want to embrace feminism out of an obligation to reject traditional values, nor do I want to embrace traditionalism out of an obligation to reject feminism.

I want the freedom to decide what makes sense for me.

That means allowing myself to be a whole person, with all the contradictions, preferences, ambitions, and uncertainties that come with being human. It means recognizing that I can change without having to justify every change in my thinking. It also means accepting that other people may arrive at conclusions very different from mine, but that does not make their choices any less valid than mine.

Ultimately, my journey through feminism, traditional values, and individualism has taught me that no ideology can fully capture the complexity of a human being.

Ideas can enlighten us. Traditions can give us a sense of belonging. Philosophies can challenge us to reconsider the way we see the world. But they should also leave room for us to think, question, and decide for ourselves.

Perhaps that is what I was searching for all along: not a definitive answer to what it means to be a woman, but the freedom to discover what being a woman means to me.

For me, that is what makes a life, and a relationship, worth building.
