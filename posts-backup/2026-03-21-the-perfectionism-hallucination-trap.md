# The Perfectionism Hallucination Trap
_Everyone’s pretending. And the pretending is what’s keeping us stuck._

[![A white dress floats on water](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1761769468235-7462f26410ef?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwzN3x8aGFsbHVjaW5hdGlvbnxlbnwwfHx8fDE3NzM5MjI5Njl8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)](<https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1761769468235-7462f26410ef?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwzN3x8aGFsbHVjaW5hdGlvbnxlbnwwfHx8fDE3NzM5MjI5Njl8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080>)Photo by [Pict4life](<https://unsplash.com/@pict4life>) on [Unsplash](<https://unsplash.com>)

Let me ask you something.

Have you ever looked at someone — a coworker, a friend, someone on LinkedIn — and thought “wow, they really have it together”? Like they just… get things right. First try. No mess. No panic. Just clean, polished, impressive output every single time.

And then you look at yourself. At your messy process. Your false starts. Your “I still don’t fully get this yet.” And you think… what’s wrong with me?

Nothing. Nothing is wrong with you.

You’re just watching everyone’s final version and comparing it to your behind-the-scenes footage.

* * *

Here’s how the trap works.

I see you looking like you’ve got it together. So I think I’m the only one who doesn’t. So I try harder to look like I do. Now YOU see ME looking like I’ve got it together. And you think you’re the only one who doesn’t. So you perform harder.

And now we’re both performing. Neither of us is okay. But we both look great.

That’s the hallucination. Everyone is pretending to be perfect for each other. And the pretending is so convincing that everyone believes everyone else is actually doing it. So the standard keeps going up. And the honesty keeps going down.

And nobody — literally nobody — stops to say “hey um… is anyone else making this up as they go?”

Yes. Everyone is. Welcome.

* * *

Think about it for a second.

Babies don’t walk on the first try. Nobody looks at a baby falling down and goes “hmm, not sure they’re cut out for this.” We just go “yay! you’ll get it!” and wait.

But somewhere between learning to walk and entering the workforce, we decided that falling down means you’re failing. That not knowing means you’re behind. That needing more than one try means something’s wrong with you.

When did we switch from “falling is part of learning” to “falling is a problem”?

I’ll tell you when. School.

School taught us that the right answer matters more than the thinking. That being wrong means a red mark, not a lesson. That the kid who got it right the first time was “smart” and the kid who needed three attempts was “struggling.”

But that’s such a weird way to measure a person. Because the kid who tried three times? They know something the first-try kid doesn’t. They know _why_ the other ways don’t work. They touched the problem from different angles. They have depth.

The first-try kid has an answer. The three-tries kid has understanding.

Guess which one is actually more prepared for real life?

* * *

And then work picks up right where school left off.

Ship fast. Get it right. Don’t let them see you struggle. Your standup is in 15 minutes — have something impressive to say. Your performance review is next month — make sure the metrics look good.

Nobody’s asking “what did you learn from the thing that didn’t work?” Nobody’s saying “tell me about a time you were completely lost and figured it out anyway.” The system rewards the appearance of getting it right. Not the actual process of figuring it out.

So we all learn the same lesson real quick. Hide the mess.

Don’t show the 47 failed attempts. Don’t mention the part where you almost gave up. Don’t talk about the three weeks where you had no idea what you were doing. Just show the final version. The clean one. The one that makes it look like you knew what you were doing the whole time.

And everyone does this. Everyone.

Your manager is doing this. Your CEO is doing this. That person on LinkedIn with the “I’m thrilled to announce” post? They’re definitely doing this. The coworker who always seems calm and prepared? They had a meltdown last Tuesday. They just didn’t tell you.

* * *

So here’s the real question.

If literally nobody gets it right the first time all the time… then what exactly are we all performing for?

Like — who started this? Who decided that the standard is perfection when perfection doesn’t exist? We’re all chasing something that no one has ever actually achieved and then feeling bad about ourselves for not achieving it.

That’s not a work culture problem. That’s a collective hallucination.

And it’s expensive. Not just for companies — for people. For their mental health. Their creativity. Their willingness to try new things. Their ability to just… be a human person who sometimes doesn’t know the answer yet.

* * *

Here’s what I’m learning though.

The moment you step outside the trap — even just a little — everything changes.

When you stop carrying the “I must get it right” energy, you move faster. Not because you’re rushing. But because you’re not frozen anymore. You’re not spending three hours perfecting something that needed to be a rough draft. You’re not avoiding the task because you’re scared it won’t be good enough. You’re just… doing it. Messy. Imperfect. Human.

And the wildest part? You actually ask for help. Because when perfection isn’t the goal, admitting you don’t know something stops being embarrassing and starts being efficient. You ask the question. You get the answer. You move on. Meanwhile the perfectionist is still Googling in silence because they’d rather spend an hour lost than 30 seconds looking like they don’t know.

Dropping perfectionism doesn’t make you sloppy. It makes you reachable. Teachable. Open. It makes you the person other people actually want to work with because you’re not pretending to have it all figured out.

* * *

I think the bravest thing you can do in a world that rewards the performance of having it all together… is to just not perform.

Say “I don’t know yet.” Say “this didn’t work but here’s what I learned.” Say “I’m still figuring it out.” And mean it. Without the apology. Without the shame face. Without the disclaimer.

Just honesty. Simple as that.

And watch how many people around you exhale. Because they were waiting for someone to go first.

* * *

But here’s the part that really got me.

Sometimes the cage door is open. And you still don’t walk out.

Someone tells you “hey, I’m not judging you. You can take your time. I’m not comparing you to anyone.” And you hear it. But you don’t believe it. Not because they’re lying. But because believing them means you’d have to actually step out and be seen. Fully. With the mess and the not-knowing and the real process on full display.

And that’s scary. Because what if you show up as your full honest self and it’s still not enough?

So it’s actually easier to not believe them. To keep assuming the room is hostile. Because then you get to stay hidden and call it survival instead of what it really is — a choice.

The perfectionism trap isn’t always external. Sometimes nobody’s putting pressure on you anymore. Sometimes the pressure is just... you. Running an old program. Protecting yourself from a rejection that hasn’t happened yet and might never happen.

And the only way to find out if the room is safe is to actually step into it. Not perfectly. Not with a plan. Just... as you are.

That’s the scariest part. And also the whole point.

* * *

And here’s what nobody tells you about stepping into the room as yourself.

It’s not just for you.

When you’re the person who says “I don’t know yet” without apologizing — someone else on the team exhales. Because they didn’t know either. They were just too scared to say it. When you ask the question everyone’s thinking but nobody’s asking — the whole room gets smarter. When you say “this didn’t work” and you’re calm about it — you just showed everyone that failure isn’t a funeral.

You don’t have to lead a workshop on vulnerability. You don’t have to give a speech. You just have to be honest in a room where everyone else is performing. That’s it.

And it’s contagious. One person being real gives another person permission. And then another. And slowly the room starts to shift. Not because someone mandated a culture change. But because one person decided to stop pretending.

That’s influence. The quiet kind. The kind that doesn’t come with a title or a promotion or a LinkedIn post. It comes from someone watching you be real and thinking “oh... I can do that too?”

And the funny thing is — the trust, the connection, the bonding — it happens naturally after that. You don’t have to engineer it. You don’t have to strategize your way into it. You just show up as a person. A real one. And other real people find you.

Sometimes the most impactful thing you can do at work isn’t shipping the perfect feature. It’s just saying “I’d love to” when someone invites you to explore.

* * *

_The trap isn’t that you’re not good enough. The trap is believing that everyone else is._
