# The Fishbowl
_Everyone could see them. No one could hear them. The work drama was about to be cinematic._

[![a person holding a goldfish in their hand](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1639241100561-01a0cb2f56b5?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHw5fHxmaXNoYm93bCUyMGF0JTIwd29ya3xlbnwwfHx8fDE3NzM5MjM3NDF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)](<https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1639241100561-01a0cb2f56b5?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHw5fHxmaXNoYm93bCUyMGF0JTIwd29ya3xlbnwwfHx8fDE3NzM5MjM3NDF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080>)Photo by [Hale Tat](<https://unsplash.com/@haletat>) on [Unsplash](<https://unsplash.com>)

Nadia was good at her job. Everybody knew it.

Creative Director at a boutique agency in Brooklyn. Twelve years in the game. The kind of person who could look at a campaign and tell you in three seconds what was wrong with it. Sharp. Fast. Respected. A little intimidating, if she was being honest. But that was the job. You had to be sharp to survive in this industry.

She’d built her reputation on taste. On quality. On never letting anything leave the studio that wasn’t _right_.

And that’s exactly why Eliot was driving her insane.

* * *

Eliot had been hired three months ago. Junior designer. Great portfolio. Clean eye. His interview was one of those rare ones where Nadia thought, _okay, this kid gets it. He sees things differently._

But something happened between the interview and the actual job.

His work was… flat. Safe. Like he was designing with oven mitts on. Every mockup he submitted looked like a template with slightly different fonts. No edge. No risk. None of the spark she’d seen in his portfolio.

And the revisions. God, the revisions. She’d give him feedback — clear, specific, not mean — and he’d come back with something almost identical. Like he hadn’t heard her. Or worse, like he’d heard her and panicked and just moved things around by two pixels hoping she wouldn’t notice.

She noticed.

* * *

“Eliot, can I grab you for five?”

He looked up from his screen like a deer hearing a branch snap. “Yeah. Sure. Of course.”

They sat in the glass meeting room. The one everyone called The Fishbowl because the whole studio could see you but not hear you. Nadia hated having hard conversations in The Fishbowl. But the other rooms were booked.

“So. The Greenline deck.”

“Yeah.” He was already nodding. Already agreeing with whatever she was about to say. “I know it’s not there yet. I’m going to fix it. I was actually going to stay late tonight and—”

“Eliot.”

He stopped.

“What happened to the guy in the interview?”

He blinked. “What do you mean?”

“In your portfolio, you had this campaign for a bike brand. Remember? The typography was wild. You’d hand-drawn the lettering and scanned it in. It was rough and imperfect and it was the best thing I saw all week. That’s why I hired you.”

He stared at the table.

“Where did that guy go?”

* * *

He didn’t answer right away. And in the silence, Nadia felt something she hadn’t expected.

She recognized him.

Not the surface of him — not the junior designer who couldn’t get the deck right. But the thing underneath. The posture. The way he agreed before she’d even finished talking. The way he pre-apologized for everything. The way his work got worse the harder he tried.

She’d seen it before.

In herself. Twelve years ago. Her first agency job. A creative director named Marcus who sighed every time she presented. Who never said “this is bad” but made you feel it in the silence. Who once looked at her mockup for eleven seconds without speaking and then said “try again” and walked away.

She’d spent that entire year trying to be what Marcus wanted. Guessing. Filtering. Playing it safe. Submitting work that wasn’t hers — it was her best guess at what would make him not sigh.

She was miserable. And her work was terrible. Not because she was terrible. But because she was so focused on not getting it wrong that she forgot how to get it right.

And here was Eliot. Doing the same thing. In her studio. Under her name on the door.

Oh.

* * *

“Can I be honest with you about something?” she said.

He braced. She could literally see him brace.

“When I was starting out, I had a boss who made me feel like every piece of work was a test. Like if I got it wrong, something bad would happen. He never yelled. He never said anything cruel. He just… evaluated. Constantly. And I got so scared of getting it wrong that I stopped trying to get it right. I just started trying to get it safe.”

Eliot was looking at her now. Actually looking.

“My work was the worst it’s ever been during that year. Not because I couldn’t do it. But because I was designing for his approval instead of for the work. I wasn’t thinking about what was good. I was thinking about what wouldn’t get me in trouble.”

She paused.

“Is that what’s happening here?”

* * *

He didn’t say yes. But his jaw did this thing where it tightened and then released. Like something was trying to come out.

“I just…” He stopped. Started again. “The stuff in my portfolio, I made that on my own. At home. No brief. No deadline. Nobody looking over my shoulder. I was just… playing. And it was easy. It came naturally.”

“And here?”

“Here I’m thinking about what you’ll think. Every time. Before I even open Illustrator, I’m already imagining presenting it to you and you telling me it’s not right. And then I freeze. And then I just… do something safe. Because safe at least won’t get me fired.”

Nadia sat back.

_Safe at least won’t get me fired._

That sentence hung in The Fishbowl like smoke.

* * *

She thought about all the feedback she’d given him. Clear. Specific. Professional. Not mean. But had she ever once said “try something weird”? Had she ever said “I’d rather see something wild that doesn’t work than something safe that does”? Had she ever told him that the mess was welcome here?

No. She’d been precise. She’d been efficient. She’d been everything Marcus was, just with better manners.

She’d created the same room. Just painted it a different color.

* * *

“Okay,” she said. “Here’s what we’re going to do. The Greenline deck is due Friday. I want you to make two versions. One that’s safe. The one you think I want. And one that’s you. The real one. The hand-drawn-typography, weird-angles, break-the-grid one. The one you’d make at 1am in your apartment with your headphones on.”

“What if it’s bad?”

“Then it’s bad and we’ll fix it together. But I want to see it.”

“What if you hate it?”

“Then I hate it and you’ll survive. And we’ll still be here on Monday.”

He almost smiled. Almost.

“Can I ask you something?” he said.

“Yeah.”

“Do you actually mean that? Like… actually?”

She thought about Marcus. About the year she lost. About the version of herself she buried to survive that room.

“Yeah, Eliot. I actually mean it.”

* * *

On Friday he presented both versions. The safe one was fine. Neat. Forgettable. Exactly what she expected.

The second one was a mess. The layout was unbalanced. The color palette was weird. One of the headlines was too big and another was slightly crooked.

But there was something in it. A texture. An energy. An idea that was trying to breathe.

“This,” Nadia said, pointing at the second one. “This is what I hired.”

“It’s kind of broken though.”

“So let’s fix it. Together.”

* * *

They spent two hours rebuilding it. Not Nadia directing and Eliot executing. Both of them at the screen, moving things, arguing about type sizes, laughing when a color combination was so ugly they couldn’t believe he’d tried it.

At one point he said, “What if we just…” and tilted the whole header thirty degrees.

“That’s unhinged,” she said.

“I know.”

“…keep it.”

He grinned. The first real one she’d seen from him in three months.

* * *

The client loved it. Not politely loved it. Actually loved it. Called it “fresh” and “brave” and “not like anything the other agencies sent over.”

But that’s not the part Nadia kept thinking about.

The part she kept thinking about was the moment Eliot said “what if we just” and didn’t flinch. Didn’t pre-apologize. Didn’t scan her face for disapproval first.

He just had an idea and said it.

That was the work. Not the deck. Not the client. Not the campaign.

Just a person in a room, not afraid to think out loud.

She’d spent twelve years becoming the kind of creative director who demanded excellence. And it took one frozen junior designer to show her that excellence doesn’t come from demand.

It comes from safety.

* * *

_She thought she was nothing like Marcus._

_She was wrong about that._

_But she was also the one who changed._

_And that made all the difference._
