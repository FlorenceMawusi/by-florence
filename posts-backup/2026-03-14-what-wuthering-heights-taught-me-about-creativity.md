# What Wuthering Heights Taught Me About Creativity
_The epiphany that made me cry _

[![person holding black and gold book](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1603049197613-366c045f11b0?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwyfHx3dXRoZXJpbmclMjBoZWlnaHRzfGVufDB8fHx8MTc3MzQzNjI1M3ww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)](<https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1603049197613-366c045f11b0?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwyfHx3dXRoZXJpbmclMjBoZWlnaHRzfGVufDB8fHx8MTc3MzQzNjI1M3ww&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080>)Photo by [Loren Cutler](<https://unsplash.com/@lorencutler>) on [Unsplash](<https://unsplash.com>)

So I watched the new _Wuthering Heights_. You know, the Emerald Fennell one with Margot Robbie and Jacob Elordi. The one that has the internet in a chokehold right now.

And listen. I didn’t go in expecting a whole spiritual awakening. I went in expecting drama, gorgeous costumes, and maybe some questionable Yorkshire accents. What I got instead was an entire framework for understanding why some people create freely and others spend their whole lives asking for permission to exist.

Let me explain.

* * *

## The slave and the master (but not like that)

So there’s this concept from Friedrich Nietzsche — master morality vs. slave morality. And before you click away because I said “Nietzsche” in a movie review, stay with me. It’s actually really simple.

The “master” isn’t about dominating people. It’s about someone who creates their own values. They decide what’s good based on what feels alive and powerful to them. They don’t need anyone to validate it. They just... want things. And they go get them.

The “slave” — and again, this isn’t about actual slavery — it’s about a mindset. The slave looks at what the master has and says “that’s evil” or “that’s wrong” or “who does she think she is?” The slave builds their whole identity around reacting to other people instead of creating from within.

Sound familiar? Yeah. It hit me too.

Professor Jiang from Predictive History on YouTube breaks this down beautifully through his game theory and civilization lectures. He talks about sovereignty — the idea that real freedom isn’t just political. It’s the ability to create, to generate new things, to not be stuck in a loop of just responding to what other people do. He connects Nietzsche’s framework to how entire societies either create or consume, and once you hear it, you can’t unhear it.

* * *

## Now let’s talk about Cathy

Margot Robbie’s Catherine Earnshaw? That’s master energy in a corset.

This woman wanted things simply because she wanted them. She didn’t need a five-year plan or a PowerPoint presentation on why her desires were valid. She just... reached. She was messy about it. She was loud about it. She was wildly impractical about it. But she never once apologized for the wanting itself.

There’s this specific energy she carries through the whole film — this child-like audacity. Like a kid who draws on the walls not because they’re being bad, but because _the wall was there and they had a crayon and why wouldn’t you?_

That’s creativity. That’s the thing.

* * *

## And then there’s the people-pleasing trap

Now flip it.

Think about every time you wanted to do something — start a project, say something bold, wear something different, chase something wild — and you stopped yourself. Because you were scared of what people would think.

That’s the slave mindset. And I don’t say that to be harsh. I say that because I lived it.

When you grow up learning that your value comes from how useful or agreeable you are to other people, you stop creating. You start performing. You stop asking “what do I want?” and start asking “what will make them happy?” And slowly, without realizing it, you hand over the one thing that actually makes you _you_ — your ability to create freely.

And the film shows you exactly what this looks like through Nelly Dean — Cathy’s maidservant (played by Hong Chau, who’s incredible in this by the way).

Nelly wanted Cathy as a best friend. She wanted closeness, connection, to matter. But as a servant, that was out of reach. She couldn’t just _say_ “I want to be your equal. I want you to see me.” That option didn’t exist for her. So what did she do instead? She got passive-aggressive. She manipulated. She moved in the shadows, pulling strings, because she didn’t believe she could just walk up and ask for what she needed directly.

And here’s the part that hit me in the chest: most of us are doing the exact same thing.

We’re not actual slaves. Most of us — at least in democratic societies — have the freedom to want things, create things, go after things, and ask for what we need directly. Nobody is stopping us. But we don’t _feel_ like we can. So instead of being direct, we get our needs met sideways. We hint instead of asking. We guilt-trip instead of communicating. We people-please and then resent people for not reading our minds.

That’s Nelly energy. And it’s because somewhere along the way, we internalized the idea that we can’t just _want_ things out loud. So we learned to get them through the back door.

Cathy never had to do any of that. Because she never questioned her **right to want.**

* * *

## The part where I cried

Here’s where it all came together for me.

I’m a creative person. I’ve always been one. I doodle on things. I write random thoughts at 2am. I build things — actual AI systems by day, and random creative experiments by night. I know what it feels like to pick up a pen and let your hand just... go. To let your conscious mind step aside and let whatever wants to come out, come out. No editing. No filtering. No “but will people like this?”

[![sliced lemon beside pink flowers](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1611087966028-bc70bc75d5f3?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHw1fHxkb29kbGluZ3xlbnwwfHx8fDE3NzM1MDE5NzF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)](<https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1611087966028-bc70bc75d5f3?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wzMDAzMzh8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHw1fHxkb29kbGluZ3xlbnwwfHx8fDE3NzM1MDE5NzF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080>)Photo by [Nechama Lock](<https://unsplash.com/@nechamalock>) on [Unsplash](<https://unsplash.com>)

That flow state? That’s the most free I’ve ever felt.

And watching this movie, something clicked. I realized that creativity isn’t just a hobby or a skill or a career path. It’s the actual mechanism of freedom. It’s how you practice being sovereign over your own life. Every time you create something — a drawing, an idea, a meal, a business, a conversation — without asking permission first, you’re exercising the most fundamental human power there is.

The ability to generate something new. From nothing. Just because you wanted to.

And then I realized: this is going to be in my life from beginning to end. Not as a phase or a side hustle or a “when I have time” thing. Creativity is literally _how I’m going to live_. It’s not separate from my career or my relationships or my growth. It **IS** the thing.

And yeah. **I cried**. Like, full tears. The ugly kind lol.

Because when you’ve spent years people-pleasing and performing and being the “good” one and making yourself smaller so other people feel comfortable — and then you realize that the opposite of all that isn’t selfishness, it’s _creation_ — it rewires something in you.

* * *

## What this means (for you, probably)

Here’s the thing everyone needs to know:

The opposite of people-pleasing is being **creative**.

The opposite of playing small is being **generative**.

The opposite of the slave mindset is having the audacity to make something new in the world just because you **felt like it.**

Nietzsche was trying to tell us this. Professor Jiang is breaking it down through game theory and history. And Emerald Fennell, whether she meant to or not, put it all in a movie with really good costumes and a Charli xcx soundtrack.

So the next time you feel that pull to create something — to write, to build, to speak, to draw, to start — and you feel that other voice saying “but what will people think?” — just remember:

That voice is the slave talking. And you don’t live there anymore.
